## Using Price Elasticity
- $$E_D = |{\frac{\text{percentage change in quantity demanded}}{\text{percentage change in price}}}|$$
- We can utilize this formula to make predictions on the percentage change in he quantity demanded
- **Total revenue**: The money a firm generates from selling its product
	- Increase in product price will correlate to more money per item sold and less total quantity sold
	- This function must be optimized to produce the greatest total revenue
- $E_d > 1 \implies$ quantity change greater (**elastic demand**)
- $E_d = 1 \implies$ price and quantity change equal (**unit elastic**)
- $E_d < 1 \implies$ price change greater (**inelastic**)
	- Generally speaking:
		- High elasticity $\implies$ inverse correlation
		- Low elasticity $\implies$ direct correlation
- $TR = Q \cdot P$
- **Cross-price elasticity** - A measure of the responsiveness of the demand in regards to changes in the price of other goods
	- $>0$ - substitutes
	- $<0$ - complements
- **Income elasticity** - The responsiveness of demand with regards to an individual's income
	- $>0$ - normal good
	- $<0$ - inferior good
- **Price elasticity of supply** - The responsiveness of the price of a given good with regard to the quantity supplied
	- Steeper slopes will result in a smaller price elasticity of supply and vice versa
- The main factors that will determine the price elasticity of supply are:
	- A rapid increase in marginal costs will result in lower price elasticity
- Why are supply curves positively sloped?
	- Short run: higher prices encourages existing firms to increase their output by purchasing more materials and hiring more workers
	- Long run: new firms entering into the market and existing firms expanding their production will result in a greater amount of total output
- **Perfectly inelastic supply** - there is a fixed amount of supply, so the elasticity will be zero
- **Perfectly elastic supply** - The elasticity will be equal to infinity
